Freshwater cultured pearls: what the name means

Natural or cultured? How a freshwater pearl grows, why the shapes vary, what the rules say in France and China, and how to care for them.

Updated September 30, 2026 · 3 min readUnder review

In brief

  • A natural pearl forms without human help; a cultured pearl, with it.
  • Freshwater cultured pearls grow in mussels, mostly in China; one mussel produces dozens.
  • Bleaching is common; in France it must be disclosed by a clearly legible notice.

Our pearls are called “freshwater cultured pearls”. Each of those words has a precise meaning.

Natural or cultured: human help

According to the DGCCRF (the French consumer protection authority), natural pearls are natural concretions formed without human intervention, while cultured pearls are formed through human intervention inside the living mollusc, by whatever means. The Laboratoire Français de Gemmologie draws the same line, as do the GIA and China’s national gem testing centre (NGTC).

One name, three usages

  • In French, “perle” or “perle fine” on its own means a natural pearl; a cultured pearl is called “perle de culture”.
  • In English, the jewellery industry’s international confederation (CIBJO) likewise keeps the word “pearl” on its own for natural pearls, and requires “freshwater cultured pearl” for pearls cultured in rivers, lakes and ponds.
  • In Chinese, it is the reverse: in the 2010 edition of the national standard GB/T 16552, “cultured pearl” (养殖珍珠) may be shortened to “pearl” (珍珠), and it is the natural pearl that must carry the word “natural” (天然). We could not check this rule in the 2017 edition now in force.

We write the full name in all three languages.

How a freshwater pearl grows

Culturing needs a technician, the GIA explains. They insert into the mollusc either a shell bead with a small piece of mantle tissue (the tissue that makes nacre), or several pieces of mantle tissue without a bead.

In fresh water the hosts are mussels, for example Hyriopsis cumingii, according to CIBJO. Pearls grown without a bead are often made almost entirely of nacre, the GIA notes, adding that freshwater pearls cultured with a bead have become increasingly common.

Each mussel produces dozens of pearls (GIA), and China is the leading source of freshwater cultured pearls (GIA).

Shapes that are rarely perfect

In 2007, a study published by the GIA noted that a Chinese method (nucleation with a flat or spherical bead) yielded mostly baroque shapes of jewellery quality, and fewer rounds and near-rounds. Freshwater pearls are also affordable, particularly in off-round shapes, according to the GIA.

What has to be disclosed

White and very light pearls, especially freshwater ones, are routinely treated: mostly by bleaching, sometimes together with optical brightening agents, according to the GIA. Other pearls may be dyed, impregnated or coated; those treatments can change over time, whereas bleaching can stay stable during normal wear (GIA).

  • CIBJO: cleaning, drilling and polishing need not be stated; bleaching, dyeing, coating, irradiation and optical brightening do.
  • France: according to the DGCCRF, if pearls have been bleached, the consumer must be told by a clearly legible notice. And the word “precious” (« précieux ») may not be used for pearls, natural or cultured.
  • China: in the 2010 edition of GB/T 16552, bleaching and brightening a cultured pearl are classed as “enhancements” (优化), dyeing and irradiation as “treatments” (处理).

Care

According to the GIA:

  • Pearl is soft (2.5 on the Mohs scale) and scratches easily: store it away from other jewellery.
  • It can be damaged by chemicals and acids: hair spray, perfume, cosmetics, even perspiration.
  • High heat can damage it.
  • No ultrasonic or steam cleaners; wipe it with a very soft, clean cloth after each wearing.

Our advice: put your bracelet on after perfume and make-up.
