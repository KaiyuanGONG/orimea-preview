The twelve animals and the five elements: where our colours come from

When the Chinese year changes, how the twelve animals relate to the five elements, which colours ancient texts give the seasons, and the rule our Atelier follows.

Updated September 30, 2026 · 4 min readUnder review

In brief

  • The official calendar changes animal at Chinese New Year; unofficial almanacs change at Lichun, around 4 February. Our Atelier follows the official calendar.
  • Each animal corresponds to an earthly branch, and each branch to one of the five elements.
  • We suggest the colours of the element that “generates” the animal’s own: a modern convention, and we present it as one.

In the Atelier, Start from an intention can suggest colours from a birth year. Here is where the correspondences we use come from, and where we had to choose.

Lichun, a point on the Sun’s path

The Chinese calendar divides the solar year into twenty-four “solar terms” (节气). The Purple Mountain Observatory (PMO, Chinese Academy of Sciences) defines them this way: starting from the spring equinox, each 15° of the Sun’s apparent movement along the ecliptic marks one term.

Lichun (立春, “the beginning of spring”) sits at a solar longitude of 315°, according to the Hong Kong Observatory. It falls around 4 February, between the 3rd and the 5th depending on the year (Xinhua). The Book of Rites, in its chapter on the monthly ordinances (月令), already links it to Wood: “某日立春，盛德在木” (“on such a day spring begins; the power of the season lies in Wood”).

Two ways to change the year

  • The official calendar changes year at Chinese New Year. The Hong Kong Observatory, which compiles the territory’s official calendar, starts the year at midnight on the first day of the first lunar month (What year is it today?). That day falls between 21 January and 20 February (PMO). In mainland China, the science outreach platform 科普中国 recalls that all official counts of years and animals start on the first day of the first month, and that the national standard on the lunar calendar (GB/T 33661-2017) sets the reference for the animal cycle.
  • Privately compiled almanacs often use Lichun instead, unlike the official calendar, the Hong Kong Observatory’s article notes, recalling that the Records of the Grand Historian already told the two apart: “正月旦，王者歲首；立春日，四時之始也” (“the first day of the first month opens the sovereign’s year; the day of Lichun opens the four seasons”).

Our choice: the Atelier follows the official calendar, so the animal changes at Chinese New Year. For a birth between New Year and Lichun, the result also shows the animal an almanac using Lichun would give. For example, in 2026 Lichun fell on 4 February and New Year on 17 February: for a birth on 10 February 2026, the Atelier shows the Snake; an almanac using Lichun would already give the Horse.

Twelve branches, twelve animals, five elements

The twelve animals are paired with the twelve “earthly branches” (地支): years with the same branch have the same animal, the Hong Kong Observatory explains, dating the use of animals to count years to the first century AD.

The Huainanzi (天文訓) sorts the heavenly stems and the earthly branches among the five elements: “甲乙寅卯，木也；丙丁巳午，火也；戊己四季，土也；庚辛申酉，金也；壬癸亥子，水也”. For Earth, the text says 四季 without naming any branches; it is usually given the remaining four (辰, 未, 戌, 丑).

The colours of the seasons

In the Book of Rites, the Son of Heaven changes his clothes and jade with each season. The Huainanzi gives each direction an emblem animal.

Element Season Clothes and jade (月令) Emblem (天文訓)
Wood spring 衣青衣，服倉玉: green-blue 蒼龍, the azure dragon
Fire summer 衣朱衣，服赤玉: red 朱鳥, the vermilion bird
Earth centre of the year 衣黃衣，服黃玉: yellow 黃龍, the yellow dragon
Metal autumn 衣白衣，服白玉: white 白虎, the white tiger
Water winter 衣黑衣，服玄玉: black 玄武, the black warrior (tortoise and snake)

Our palettes follow these colours, widened as many current sources do (gold and silver with Metal, brown with Earth, deep blue with Water). Many modern sources also group orange, pink and purple with Fire; those colours do not appear in the Book of Rites.

The generating cycle

The Huainanzi also sets out a cycle: “水生木，木生火，火生土，土生金，金生水” (Water generates Wood, Wood Fire, Fire Earth, Earth Metal, Metal Water).

An article published in 2018 on Sina Art uses it to pair each animal with a stone of the element that generates its own: the Monkey and the Rooster (Metal) get citrine, yellow like Earth, because “土生金” (Earth generates Metal). This is a modern commercial convention, not an ancient rule. The page promises effects; we keep only the colour logic.

Two stones in the article are not in our range: we replace them with materials in the same element’s colours. For Earth, we add garnet, red, the colour of Fire in the Book of Rites.
