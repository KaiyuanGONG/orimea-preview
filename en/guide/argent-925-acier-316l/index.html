925 silver and 316L steel: what they are, how to care for them

What “925” means, why silver darkens and how to store it, what 316L steel is, the EU rule on nickel, and a few habits for elastic cord.

Updated September 30, 2026 · 3 min readUnder review

In brief

  • “925” means 925 parts per thousand of silver; the rest is usually copper.
  • Silver darkens on contact with sulphur compounds in the air: a closed bag slows it down, a silver cloth cleans it gently.
  • 316L steel resists corrosion thanks to a thin chromium oxide film. Like any jewellery, it must meet the EU limit on nickel release.

Our stone and pearl lines use 925 silver: beads, tubes, discs, chains and half-bangles. The glass and ceramic line uses 316L stainless steel. Most bracelets are strung on elastic cord. All of these parts are virtual for now.

What “925” means

The fineness of a precious metal is expressed in parts per thousand, the London Assay Office, one of the UK’s assay offices, explains; for silver, the standards recognised under the international hallmarking convention are 800, 925 and 999. So 925 silver is 92.5% silver. According to the GIA, the remaining 7.5% is usually copper, sometimes other metals such as nickel.

In France, a piece of jewellery may only be called “argent” (silver) if it is at least 800 parts per thousand, according to French customs (Douane). According to a table of hallmarks published by the Douane in 2009 (an old document, to be confirmed), the 925 and 800 standards are marked with a head of Minerva.

Why silver darkens

The dark layer that forms on silver is mainly black silver sulphide. It is caused by sulphur compounds in the air, such as hydrogen sulphide, the Canadian Conservation Institute (CCI) explains. Among the sources of these compounds, the CCI lists certain foods (eggs, mayonnaise), rubber and some paints. Salts and oils from the skin can also mark silver.

Storing and cleaning silver

The CCI’s advice is written for museum objects; here is how we adapt it to jewellery.

  • Store: wrap the bracelet in acid-free (unbuffered) tissue paper, then put it in a resealable polyethylene bag. Anti-tarnish cloths that contain tiny silver particles are, according to the CCI, particularly effective.
  • Keep away from rubber bands.
  • Clean with a silver polishing cloth, which is gentler than a liquid or paste polish because it contains less abrasive. Never use a multi-purpose metal polish.
  • On a strung bracelet, our advice: rub only the silver parts, without touching the pearls and stones.

316L steel

Steel becomes stainless when it contains more than 10.5% chromium, the worldstainless association explains: a thin, chromium-rich oxide film forms on its own at the surface and re-forms when exposed to oxygen.

According to a manufacturer’s datasheet, 316L steel typically contains 17.2% chromium, 10.1% nickel, 2.1% molybdenum and 0.02% carbon. The “L” stands for low carbon, which improves resistance to some forms of corrosion; molybdenum improves its behaviour in many acidic environments.

We found no authoritative guidance on caring for steel jewellery. Our cautious advice: a soft, dry cloth.

Nickel: an EU limit

The EU’s REACH regulation limits how much nickel a piece of jewellery may release (Regulation No 552/2009, restated by the French DGCCRF):

Article Nickel release limit
Necklaces, bracelets, chains, rings and other articles in direct and prolonged contact with the skin 0.5 µg per cm² per week
Posts inserted into pierced ears or other pierced parts of the body 0.2 µg per cm² per week

A coating must keep within the limit for at least two years of normal use, and conformity is checked with the test methods of the European standards body (CEN).

316L steel contains about 10% nickel, and 925 silver may contain a little. The composition alone does not tell how much nickel a part releases: only a test does.

Elastic cord

There is no standard on this; here is practical advice from a bead supplier and a jewellery maker:

  • Roll the bracelet over your hand rather than stretching it (both sources).
  • Keep it away from chemicals, oils, perfume and sunlight, which wear the elastic down over time (the supplier).
  • Our advice: take it off to swim, shower, exercise or do the washing-up.

At ORIMEA

  • Before any sale, we will ask suppliers for the silver’s fineness and hallmarks, and for a declaration on nickel release based on tests.
  • We do not write “hypoallergenic” or “nickel-free”.
  • Do you have a known sensitivity to metals? Write to us before reserving and we will tell you the exact composition of each part.
