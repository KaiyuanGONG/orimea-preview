Where does the birthstone list come from?

The “official” list was adopted in 1912 by American jewellers, then revised in 1952, 2002 and 2016. Its history, the British variant, and the choices we made.

Updated September 29, 2026 · 3 min readUnder review

In brief

  • The modern list dates from 1912: an American jewellers’ association, today Jewelers of America, drew it up.
  • It was extended in 1952, 2002 and 2016; the United Kingdom has had its own list since 1937.
  • For April, May, July and September we use a stone from the British list; October exists here only as a colour version.

Giving someone the stone of their birth month is a well-established custom in jewellery. Yet the list people quote today is recent and far from universal: it was drawn up by the trade, then changed several times.

An old idea

The idea of one stone per month is old. The historian Josephus, in the first century, linked the twelve stones of the high priest’s breastplate, described in the Book of Exodus, with the twelve months and the twelve signs of the zodiac. Saint Jerome, citing Josephus, later suggested to Christians the twelve foundation stones of the New Jerusalem (Revelation, chapter 21).

The custom of wearing the stone of one’s own month came later, and its origin is disputed: the mineralogist George F. Kunz placed it in eighteenth-century Poland, the GIA in 1560s Germany. These points come from a secondary source (Wikipedia); we have not checked them against the original texts.

1912: a jewellers’ list

The so-called “official” list dates from 1912. According to Jewelers of America, it was drawn up by the American National Retail Jewelers Association, which became Jewelers of America. It is a trade convention, not a religious text or a scientific classification.

It was later extended, according to National Jeweler:

Year Change
1952 Alexandrite (June), citrine (November) and tourmaline (October) added; zircon appears in December (replacing lapis lazuli, according to Wikipedia)
2002 Tanzanite (December) added
2016 Spinel (August) added

The British list

According to Wikipedia, the United Kingdom’s National Association of Goldsmiths published its own list in 1937. The encyclopedia also gives a “UK (2013)” version which, alongside the precious stones, offers clear quartz in April, chrysoprase in May, carnelian in July and lapis lazuli in September. We have not found the original document for this 2013 version.

The table we use

For each month we start from the current American list. Where it offers only a precious stone that we do not offer as beads (diamond, emerald, ruby, sapphire), we take the stone from the British list.

February amethyst amethyst violet glass

Two notes:

  • October: we have neither opal nor tourmaline in our range for now. October exists here only in opaline glass, which is glass, not opal.
  • Colour versions: every month also exists in glass or ceramic of the same colour. These are colours, not stones: we attach no meaning to them.

All the beads listed are virtual for now (indicative images, prices to be confirmed).

A convention, not a promise

In its current form, the birthstone list is a traders’ convention, adjusted over the decades. We offer it as a starting point for choosing colours: in the Atelier, Start from an intention gives the stone of your month, its colour version and the rule used. All our rules are set out in How we talk about meaning.

These associations come from traditions and beliefs; they have no proven effect. No stone heals or guarantees any outcome.
